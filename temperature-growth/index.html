Does a hot year make a country poorer?

Hot countries tend to be poor. But is that because of the heat, or because of everything else that differs between, say, Niger and Norway? This page follows one influential answer and checks whether it still holds with newer data.

The paper in 60 seconds

  • Question. Do higher temperatures slow down economic growth, and does that depend on how rich a country is?
  • Trick. Instead of comparing hot countries with cold ones, the authors compare each country with itself: was growth lower in years that were hotter than that country's normal?
  • Finding. In poor countries, a year 1 °C warmer than usual cut growth by about 1.3 percentage points. That is a lot when typical growth is about 1 % a year. In rich countries there was no clear effect.
  • Our check. With the authors' own data we get the same result. With newer temperature and GDP data the effect in poor countries is about half as large and less certain. Whether it has faded since 2003 is unclear.

Hot countries are poorer, but that alone proves little

Each dot is a country. Further right means richer, higher up means hotter. The pattern is clear: the hottest countries are mostly poor. But hot countries also differ in history, institutions, disease and geography, so this picture alone cannot tell us what heat itself does.

The grey bars in the last view show how far temperature swings within one country from year to year. That year-to-year swing is what the paper uses.

Follow one country through hot and cool years

The authors use each country as its own comparison. Pick a country. The bars show how much warmer (above zero) or cooler (below zero) each year was than that country's long-run average. The line shows how fast the economy grew that year.

Don't be surprised if a single country shows the opposite of the main result. Over six decades most countries got warmer and their growth changed for many other reasons (wars, reforms, commodity booms). In one country these long-run trends can swamp the effect of a hot year. That is why the paper pools all countries and compares each year only with other countries in the same region in that same year.

The main result: hot years hurt poor countries

Pooling every country and year, the chart shows how much one year that is 1 °C hotter than normal changes growth, separately for rich and poor countries. The bar is the 95 % confidence interval: if it crosses zero, we cannot rule out "no effect". Switch between the original data and our updated data.

Change the data, and the effect shrinks

Test yourself: three exam-style questions

From easy to hard. Try to answer each one before opening the solution.

Question 1 · warm-upWhy don't the authors simply regress growth on each country's average temperature across countries?

That comparison suffers from omitted variable bias. Things like institutions, colonial history, disease burden or distance to markets affect growth and are correlated with climate. Write the true model as y = βT + γW + ε, where W is, for example, institutional quality. Then the cross-country estimate converges to β + γ·δ, where δ is the slope from regressing W on T. Better institutions raise growth (γ > 0) and are less common in hot places (δ < 0). So the bias is negative, and a cross-country regression would overstate how harmful heat is.

Country fixed effects remove anything about a country that does not change over time, including its average climate. The remaining variation is year-to-year deviation from each country's own normal. Weather in a given year is plausibly random, so it is unlikely to be correlated with the omitted factors.

Question 2 · intermediateAfter removing all the fixed effects, the standard deviation of temperature falls from about 7 °C to under 0.4 °C. Why does this make measurement error a bigger worry? We instrument the paper's temperature with an independent dataset (CRU). What does the result tell us?

With classical measurement error, OLS is biased towards zero by the factor Var(T*) / (Var(T*) + Var(e)). Fixed effects remove most of the true signal Var(T*), but the station and interpolation noise Var(e) mostly stays. So the noise share rises, and so does the attenuation.

If two datasets measure the same true temperature with independent errors, instrumenting one with the other removes this bias. On the same sample the poor-country effect is with OLS and with IV. The first stage is very strong. The IV estimate is not larger in absolute value, so attenuation from measurement error does not seem to be a big problem. One caveat: both datasets are built from largely the same weather stations, so their errors are probably correlated. In that case IV only partly removes the bias.

Question 3 · hardWith newer temperature and GDP data for the same years (1961–2003), the poor-country effect falls from about −1.35 to about −0.6 and is no longer significant. Does this show that the original result was wrong? Discuss which kind of data error could cause each change.

Not necessarily. Think about where the error enters.

  • Error in growth (the outcome). If GDP revisions are pure noise, they make estimates less precise but do not bias β. They only cause bias if the revisions are correlated with temperature shocks. For example, statistical offices in poor, agricultural economies might first record a hot-year harvest failure and then revise it away. In that case one of the two vintages is biased, and we cannot tell which from these data alone.
  • Error in temperature (the regressor). Different temperature products would attenuate the estimate (Question 2), but the IV check suggests this effect is small. Weighting matters more. Population-weighted temperature measures heat where people work, while area weighting gives deserts and mountains equal weight. A smaller effect with area weighting is what we would expect if heat hurts through people and farms. This is a reason to prefer the paper's weighting, not evidence against it.
  • Sampling uncertainty. The confidence intervals of the original and new estimates overlap. The difference itself may not be statistically significant.

A fair conclusion: the direction of the effect is robust, but its size depends on data choices. Policy numbers based on "−1.3 pp per degree" should be treated as an upper estimate.
